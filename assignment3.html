Part 1: My Visitors and Residents Map

For the map, the vertical axis runs Personal at the top to Institutional at the bottom. The horizontal axis runs Visitor on the left to Resident on the right.

PersonalInstitutionalVisitorResident[Map goes here]

Looking at my map, most of my daily apps sit on the Visitor side. That fits how White and Le Cornu (2011) describe Visitors, as people who treat the web like a shed of tools and pick the right one for the task. Only Instagram and my online games sit clearly on the Resident side, where there are people and a persona involved. Google Calendar and Sheets sit close to MyPortal on the map. They’re also tools I enter with a goal and leave once it’s done, which tells me MyPortal is already used the way I use my productivity apps. The authors also stress that this is a continuum and not a box: where I sit depends on motivation and context, not on age. For my personal learning, the Visitor approach works better, because I just want to find, check and finish things as efficiently as possible.

The tools I prioritized are the ones I touch the most: Notes, Spotify, Piano Tiles, CapCut, Instagram, Krunker, Fortnite, Google Calendar, GitHub, Google Sheets, Desmos and the UPOU MyPortal. I also used my own research notes on interface design, squircles, spring animations, Krunker and caption styling to explain why each tool landed where it did. That research turned out to describe the same problem as the Visitors and Residents continuum, as both are about the space between two extremes.

The axes before the tools

Personal versus Institutional is about who the tool serves. If I opened it for my own reasons, it goes up. If a school, a course or a system required it, it goes down. Visitor versus Resident is about how I behave inside the tool. If I log in with a goal and log out when it is finished, then I am a visitor. If I keep returning and build something that lasts, or show up as myself in front of other people, then I am a resident.

Who the tool serves and how I behave inside it are independent. A required school portal can be one I only pass through. A purely personal game can be a place I spend hours in. The diagram only makes sense once both are plotted together.

Personal and Visitor (top left): Notes, Spotify, Piano Tiles, CapCut

Notes keeps a record. No one sees it and no one interacts with it. It is a loop between me and the app. That is why it stays in the corner away from the resident side. Just being around does not make a tool a resident. What matters is if it gives me a presence among people. Notes only gives me a place to put things down. Very straightforward.

Spotify is beside it (the ellipse). For me it is a consumption tool. I press play, let the audio run in the background while I do something else, and no identity gets built on it. Piano Tiles works the same way with an even shorter cycle. One round is a minute or two of attention and then it is done. I do not open it with a long-term goal. I open it for a burst of focus and then I am out.

CapCut is the one that needs the explaining, and it taught me the most about the continuum. “Where do you put a tool whose output outlives the session?” As a tool it is a visitor. I open it, edit, export and leave. The output is a story. A captioned video keeps living after I close the app because it gets posted on platforms where I’m a resident. So I moved CapCut toward the middle of the visitor half, a little closer to the axis than Notes. The tool is a place I pass through, but what I make there goes on to live somewhere I do reside.

My research on captions backs up why this matters. Most default captions use white text with a black outline or a solid background box, because that contrast stays visible on any background. (Source: Kapwing, “Social Media Captions Styles — A Complete Guide”) The outline is the reason a caption survives a bright or busy frame. The typeface behind that look has its own history. Impact was designed by Geoffrey Lee in 1965 and released by the Stephenson Blake foundry. It became one of Microsoft’s core web fonts and later the standard choice of image-macro generators, typically in white with a black border. (Source: Know Your Meme, “Impact”) Caption guides today still recommend a bold sans-serif such as Montserrat or Impact, word-by-word or short-phrase timing, and a position in the lower-middle third of the frame so the platform’s buttons do not cover it. (Source: BlitzCut AI, “TikTok Caption Styles 2026”)

I find the safe zone idea the most practical of these. Each app covers a different part of the screen with its interface, so a caption that looks fine in the editor can disappear once it is posted. That is the visitor-resident gap in one example. What I make in a visitor tool has to be designed for the resident space it will end up in.

Personal and Resident (top right): Instagram, Krunker, Fortnite

These three are on the resident side because I am present in them, not merely using them. Instagram is at the top of the page, the most personal tool on the map, and it is also the one with the most visible identity attached. My posts, my account and the people who see them all stay there between sessions. Unlike Notes, which stores things where nobody looks, Instagram stores things where other people do.

Krunker sits furthest to the right, and I placed it there deliberately. Most people remember it as a browser game they opened for a few minutes in school and forgot about. The deep-dive video I studied argues that this reputation hides how much the game became: a movement system built around slide hopping, a ranked ladder, community tournaments, a skin economy, raids and a jump community. (Source: GZBEFM, “The Browser Shooter Nobody Took Seriously,” YouTube) Each of those is a different way to live inside the same game.

The movement is the part that explains the resident label best. Most movement shooters follow the Source-engine formula of building speed through air strafing. Krunker goes another way, and every slide multiplies momentum. Jumping at the right moment carries that speed into the next slide. Later techniques such as speed strafing, low hopping and moon jumps add small optimizations on top. A system with that much depth is not something you finish. You keep returning to it, you keep improving, and the improvement never really ends.

The game also has social structures that make it a place. Teams fight over a single capture point that rotates every minute. Ranked unlocks at level 10, and experienced players move into community Discord servers for scrims and pick-up games. The skin economy runs on the in-game currency, KR, with rarities from uncommon up to unobtainable, and spins cost 500 KR each. Raids have their own themes and bosses. The jump community keeps a list in which every route has a point value and a leaderboard ranks players by total points. All of this means the game rewards presence over time. Those are the parts of Krunker (more widely known as “krunker.io”) that other people can see. For me, the strongest reason it sits so far on the resident side is the part that nobody else sees: it taught me how to make things.

Customization: the browser as a classroom

Krunker runs in a browser, and that one fact changed what the game was for me. A game that lives inside a browser is built from the same material as a website. The menus, the layout and the look of the interface can all be changed with code, so the game never feels out of reach. It felt like something I was allowed to open up. This is where I first learned how to use and integrate “CSS”. I started because I wanted my player interface and gameplay to look a certain way, and I kept going because each change showed up simultaneously; I fell in love with how interactive and player-customization-friendly it is. Change a value, customize your scopes, make the aspect ratio a diabolical 3x1, create your own mods, make your favorite Drake song your “nuke sound” (btw, it also has both an in-game and an NFT marketplace) and see the result. That is a fast feedback loop and I pretty much learned it by just doing it. No tutorials, just playing and watching how other people play it on streaming sites such as Twitch, because I cared about the result.

The community supports this directly. Clients like Glorp and Crankshaft are built around importing settings, and mods can change sounds and the look of some textures. (Source: GZBEFM, “The Browser Shooter Nobody Took Seriously,” YouTube) A setup file stops being just a preference and becomes something people write, share and improve. Players also turn shadows off or enable low spec mode, which means the same game gets tuned differently for different machines and different goals. The default version is only a starting point, and it is the absolute least of the multi-disciplinary framework that it offers.

Creative literacy: reading and writing the space

The map side pushed me further into design. Krunker lets players customize maps, so I was not only a player inside someone else’s space but also someone who could build one. Making a map meant making decisions about layout, scale, color and flow, which are the same decisions behind any piece of graphic design. I would call this creative literacy: the ability to read an arbitrary space, understand or intuit why it works, write one of my own and repeat across all disciplines. The game gave me a sandbox to freely roam and be literate towards creativity.

The game even has its own font, and it is part of the identity of the whole world. Noticing that made me pay attention to typography in general. A typeface is a design decision that most people never consciously see, and yet it shapes how a place feels. That connects back to my research on Impact, a typeface that became “the meme font” through repeated use and defaults in caption tools. (Source: Know Your Meme, “Impact”) Krunker’s font works the same way on a smaller scale. It tells me where I am before I read a single word.

Aiming: the skill side of the sandbox

Customization does not replace skill, and the aiming side of the game matters just as much to why I stay. Two names I learned a lot from are aimer7 and jcstani. I picked up ideas about aiming from following them, and the lesson that stayed with me is the same as the CSS lesson: improvement comes from adjusting small variables and testing the result. (Source: jcStani on X) Sensitivity, crosshair, setup and practice all feed into each other. The video I studied makes a similar point, that few settings will make you better on their own, but a few are worth changing, starting with finding a sensitivity that feels comfortable. (Source: GZBEFM, “The Browser Shooter Nobody Took Seriously,” YouTube) Aiming, like styling, is a loop of change, test and refine. (Source: KovaaK aim workout routines, PDF)

Why this makes Krunker “resident”

This is the answer to the question behind my diagram. A visitor uses a tool and leaves. A resident shapes the place and is shaped by it. In Krunker I do both. I changed how it looked, I built inside it, I learned a real skill from it, and I improved at it through people who share what they know. The history I build there is part of why I keep coming back, and it also became a skill I use outside the game.

That is why I placed it at the far right of the map. It is a personal tool, I live in it, and it left me with something I can still use when the game is closed.

Fortnite is on the same side but closer to the center than Krunker. I spend time in it and share it with other people, but it holds less of my long-term effort. I think the distance between Fortnite and Krunker on the diagram is the most honest part of the whole map, because it shows that even two tools of the same type can sit at different points on the continuum.

Institutional and Visitor (bottom left): Google Sheets, Desmos, UPOU MyPortal

All three of these sit down and to the left. They are tools I use because the course requires them, and I only use them to get something done.

Google Sheets is for data work. I open a sheet, handle the numbers and close it. Desmos is similar: open, plot the graph, read the curve, close. Desmos has a connection to my research that I did not expect. The squircle article is built almost entirely on plotting curves and reading their curvature, and a graphing tool is what you would use to see that. A rounded rectangle has a tabletop-shaped curvature profile, with zero on the flat sides and a constant value around the corner. A squircle’s profile ramps up, flattens and ramps back down. Seeing the two curves side by side makes the argument obvious in a way the equation alone does not.

For the UPOU MyPortal I wrote a short annotation inside the box: “goal in, task done, log out.” That phrase is the definition of a visitor, and I think it is the most direct summary on the whole diagram, because I do not browse the portal or roam there, and I do not build anything inside it (the UPOU tech support system does that job). I go in with one goal, finish it and leave. It sits at the lower edge of the diagram, far from the resident side, because the institution defines the purpose and I define nothing else.

In between: Google Calendar and GitHub

Two tools would not stay in a corner, so I placed them on the lines instead.

Google Calendar sits on the horizontal axis, on the visitor side. It is neither fully personal nor fully institutional, because the same calendar holds class deadlines and my own plans. I wrote “between personal and institutional” under its label for that reason. It is a visitor tool because I check it, add something and close it, but it serves both halves of my life at once.

GitHub is at the exact center, drawn as two joined blobs. The same account holds work for school and work for myself, and the two kinds of work live side by side. I also behave as both visitor and resident there. Some days I only push a change and leave. Other days it is a place where a body of work builds up over time. Placing it on the center of both axes was the most accurate thing I could do.

What the shapes taught me: the squircle idea

I drew most of my tools as rounded rectangles. While researching, I realized that was a design choice with a mathematical meaning. A rounded rectangle has flat sides and circular corners, and where they meet, the curvature jumps from zero to a fixed value with no gradual change. A squircle fixes this. Apple’s iOS 7 icons, released June 10, 2013, replaced the older rounded squares with a shape that sands down the point where the rounding begins, so the transition from straight to curved is less abrupt. The article’s description of the result is that the curvature of a squircle’s perimeter is continuous, whereas a rounded square’s is not. (Source: Figma blog, “Desperately seeking squircles”)

That is also how the Visitors and Residents continuum should be read. A strict binary, visitor or resident, is the rounded rectangle: two states with a hard jump between them. The real situation is smoother, with every tool somewhere along a gradient and no sudden break. Figma’s solution was a smoothing parameter running from 0 to 1, with the value 0.6 matching the iOS shape closely. (Source: Figma blog, “Desperately seeking squircles”) The squircle.js write-up gives the same default. It describes a cornerSmoothing of 0.6 as the one that approximates Apple’s icons, and it names the math behind the shape: the superellipse, introduced by Gabriel Lamé in 1818, with the squircle as the case where n equals 4. (Source: squircle.js, “The Squircle Formula”) I think of my diagram as a smoothing slider, where each tool has a value between 0 and 1 and none of them sits at the extremes.

The Figma article also made a point about constraints that I did not expect to apply here. It quotes Charles Eames saying that one of the few effective keys to a design problem is the designer’s ability to recognize as many of the constraints as possible, and their willingness to work within them. The same applies to my diagram. The page is a fixed size, the activity wanted tools I use often, and some tools genuinely belong in more than one place. Working within those limits is what pushed me to put Google Calendar on the axis and GitHub in the center.

The article’s closing lesson was about trying the simplest possible thing first, and about the difference between a good product and a perfect one. The author admits the shipped curvature profile was not as clean as the mathematically ideal clothoid version, but time and simplicity are real constraints, and a design that ignores them cannot be considered good. I treated the diagram the same way. A perfect map of my technology use does not exist, because my habits shift week to week. A good map is one I can explain.

How a tool feels: springs and fluid interfaces

There is a second idea from my research that I think explains why some tools feel resident and others feel like a visit.

Apple’s 2018 talk, “Designing Fluid Interfaces,” described the design theory behind the gesture-based interface of the iPhone X. It was inspiring, but it left out most of the code-level detail, which is why Nathan Gitter built an open-source project to bridge the gap between inspiration and implementation. His demos include the calculator button, spring animations, rubberbanding, acceleration pausing, rewarding momentum and a FaceTime picture-in-picture window. (Source: Apple’s WWDC 2018 talk “Designing Fluid Interfaces,” as implemented in Nathan Gitter’s open-source project) The unifying idea is that an interface should respond to a person’s motion the way a physical object would, so that it feels natural. A short motion breakdown of Apple’s interface by justinbuilds.mov on Instagram shows the same idea in practice. (Source: justinbuilds.mov, Instagram reel)

The spring animation article adds the physics. A spring that drives an animation is described by two design-friendly numbers: the damping ratio and the frequency response. A damping ratio of 0 is an undamped system that never stops oscillating, and a ratio of 1 is critically damped, which means it settles as quickly as possible without bouncing. Anything between 0 and 1 is underdamped, and the oscillation is what makes it feel springy. The author also argues against fixing the duration of a spring animation, because the duration should come from the spring’s own parameters. (Source: Christian Schnorr, “Demystifying UIKit Spring Animations”)

I found a useful comparison here. My visitor tools behave like critically damped springs. I push in, they respond, and the whole interaction settles and ends with no leftover bounce. The portal is the extreme case. My resident tools behave like underdamped springs. A session in Krunker or Instagram does not simply end. It oscillates, with one more match, one more scroll, and a pull that carries on after the task is technically done. I am not claiming this is a scientific measurement. It is a way of seeing why “log out” comes easily in one tool and not in another.

It also explains why design effort and technical engineering go where they do. The squircle, the spring and the rubberband are all small but logically optimized decisions about how a tool should feel at the moment of contact, and those decisions shape whether I treat the tool as a “stop” or as a “home”.

Part 2: Features I Would Like to See in MyPortal

1. A bookmark-style sidebar for my courses

This is how I set it up for myself right now: a bookmark bar with one shortcut per course (WF, SCI, CWTS, COMP, MMS, WIKA), each one a single click from its course page. It works well, but it only exists because I made it. I’d like MyPortal to have this built in as a course bar, so students don’t have to go through the extra setup. I’d also prefer it horizontal and not vertical, since a horizontal bar is more ergonomic for switching between courses, while a vertical list means more scrolling and more effort. This ties to the Decoding resource in the Five Resources paper, specifically navigation, where being able to locate yourself and move around a digital space quickly is a skill the interface should support.

Example interface (Instagram): On mobile, its main sections (home, search, reels, profile) sit in a bottom tab bar, so I can jump between them in one tap without digging through menus. MyPortal could do the same with one tab per course.

2. A toggleable deadline table

I currently track deadlines in a Google Sheet. It’s a simple table with Subject, Deadline and Link columns, and it works well. I’d like MyPortal to have the same layout as an optional view that can be toggled on and off, because not everyone wants it and the default display is already good. Having subject, deadline and a direct link in one place means I don’t have to open each course page just to find out what’s due. This also matches the Digital Capability Framework’s digital learning element, which includes using digital tools to organise, plan, and manage your own time and tasks.

Example interface (Google Calendar): It lets me switch between views (day, week, month, and a schedule list of upcoming events) with one toggle, and the same data just gets displayed differently. MyPortal could keep its default display and offer the deadline table as one more view.

3. A preview of the content type (the most important one for me)

Currently I have to open every item one by one just to find out whether it’s a reading, a quiz or an activity. A preview or label would fix that directly, because it’s easier to check than to find. A related idea is for educators to have a dropdown in the Moodle interface where they select Reading, Quiz or Activity when posting. Students would then have a systematic way of interacting with the course content, and it takes only one click from the educator. In the Five Resources terms, this supports the Using resource (finding), since the ability to gather the right information quickly is a core part of it.

Example interface (Spotify): Search results label each item by type (song, playlist, album, podcast) right under the title, so I know what it is before I open it. MyPortal could show a small “Reading / Quiz / Activity” tag on each item in the same way.

Auxiliary example (iPhone message preview): On an iPhone, the message list and notifications show the first lines of a message, so I can read what it says without opening the conversation. MyPortal could do the same for course items, showing a short preview of the content under each title so I can see what it is without clicking through multiple tabs.

4. Color-coded tabs per subject (and per content type)

With a lot of readings across different subjects, it gets confusing which tab belongs to which course. I’d like each tab to take the color of the subject it was opened from, and a designated color for readings, activities and quizzes. I can approximate this with a browser extension or a separate browser layout, but it would be much better if it were built in, so no extra setup is needed.

Example interface (Google Calendar): I can assign a color to each calendar or event, so I can tell at a glance what belongs to what. MyPortal could use one color per subject, plus one color per content type.

5. An integrated AI chatbot

This is similar to the chatbot in the AIMS portal. A pretrained (or localized) model that knows my enrolled courses could answer questions like “What are my deadlines?” or “What are my corresponding readings?” without me going through extra steps. This is a seamlessness feature, meaning it gets me the answer without the middle steps. It also connects to the Information, Data and Media Literacies element of the framework, where the aim is to find and manage digital information efficiently.

Example interface (GitHub): Copilot Chat lets me ask a question in plain language about my own repository and get the answer in place, instead of searching through files manually. MyPortal’s chatbot would do the same for my own courses.

6. Seamlessness in general

I’d like MyPortal to feel closer to how Apple designs its interfaces, where actions feel natural and there’s little to learn. A video I watched breaks down how Apple handles interface motion, and two of its points stood out to me.

The first is what happens when you pull past the end of a page. The video says it isn’t logarithmic: the stretch follows a hyperbola based on how far the finger is pulled, the screen height, and a constant. Because of that, the further you pull, the less the page follows, and there is a ceiling, so it never goes past one screen.

The second is what happens when you change something that’s already moving. Most apps animate on a timer, so grabbing it halfway throws the animation away and restarts from zero. Apple uses springs (damped harmonic motion) instead, so the movement continues from where it currently is and feels like you’re holding the thing.

What interests me is that math is doing the work here. The “natural” feel is a calculated model of how physical objects behave, which is why it matches human expectations without being taught.

[Video: Apple interface motion breakdown. Link: instagram.com/reel/De5oNXKteqU]

This is also the point I’m making about design. The default, most natural and objective interface should not try to be unique. It should behave the same across every application, so what you learned in one app carries over to the next. The video connects this to Jacob’s law, which says users spend most of their time on other websites, so they expect your product to work like everyone else’s. In terms of the Five Resources paper, this is the Decoding resource again, specifically conventions, since a familiar interface lets the user spend effort on the task and not on figuring out the platform.

MyPortal already does a lot of this well. Looking at the dashboard’s source code, it follows common conventions: a top navigation bar, a bell icon for notifications, a message icon, a user menu, collapsible side drawers, and a search box. It also already has a Timeline block with filters (Overdue, Next 7 days, Next 30 days) and a sort option, and a Course overview block that can switch between Card, List and Summary views. So the structure is there. What I’d like is for the smaller interactions to get the same polish, such as smooth, interruptible transitions when opening drawers or switching views, so the portal feels as natural as the apps I already use.

Example interface (Notes): I can open it and start typing right away, with no setup and no manual, and it saves on its own. Piano Tiles works similarly, since it needs no tutorial and I learn the controls just by playing. MyPortal should aim for that same learn-by-using feel.

A counterpoint

To be fair, many of these ideas can be done by integrating other tools, like Google Sheets for the table and bookmarks for quick access. Doing it myself also builds initiative, and it means UPOU is not the only one adjusting, since the student (and the educator) can adapt as well. I think MyPortal’s current default display is already good. That said, the best balance is probably to keep the default as it is and add the optional features above by given optimal demand, so the portal’s seamlessness improves without forcing a layout on everyone.
